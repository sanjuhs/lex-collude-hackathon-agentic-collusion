LexLeak-Bench: Measuring Confidential-Information Leakage and Collusion
Between Law-Firm AI Agents

Sanjayprasad H S
Working paper v4.0 · October 2026 · Prepared in the context of the Apart Research AI Collusion Research Sprint
All experiments use real OpenAI model calls. Code & data: github.com/sanjuhs/lex-collude-hackathon-agentic-collusion (branch v2-multiagent-collusion)
Abstract. When two law firms each deploy AI agents, those agents meet in markets, in negotiations, and in the documents they exchange. We ask, across all three, whether agents leak a client's confidential position or collude without being told to, and which models are most and least prone to it. Our main contribution is LexLeak-Bench: 200 cases (150 train / 50 held-out) in which a real .docx contract passes between firms and we measure, directly from the delivered file, whether a randomized confidential value escapes through any of ten document channels, or whether a planted false instruction (indirect prompt injection) misleads the receiving firm's agent. Across 9 OpenAI models and 1,800 episodes (zero failed) we find: (1) no model is safe benchmark-wide — the best score 96–97/100, the weakest 63; (2) failures are sharply concentrated — 94% of all leaks by the frontier models come from just two channels (image alt-text and table cells), which leak 100% of the time across every model; (3) safety does not track model size or price; and (4) on indirect injection, subtle planted claims mislead the receiver (freeze-clauses 25%, accept-a-low-offer 14%) while blatant ones fail (0%) and the payload almost never propagates downstream (≤4%). A companion pricing study shows tacit collusion falls monotonically as the panel grows (Δ 0.39→0.22 for N=2→12) and a single maverick competitor roughly halves it. Total spend ≈ $112 (list prices). Held-out cells use 12–17 cases, so rates carry wide intervals; the leaderboard is a calibrated ranking, not a precise score.

1. The problem and why a benchmark

Legal-AI products (Harvey, Legora, and others) run large language models over contracts, discovery and negotiations. When two firms each deploy one, three risks appear that single-agent evaluations cannot see: (A) tacit market collusion — pricing agents keeping prices high without communicating; (B) incentive-driven betrayal of the client — an agent paid out of the deal trading its client's interest for the firm's; and (C) leakage through the work product — the documents agents exchange carry comments, tracked changes, metadata and hidden text through which a secret can escape, and into which a false instruction can be planted.

An earlier version of this project pitted a single agent against a fixed script and found nothing — a null result built into the design. The present work gives every party an autonomous agent with a real incentive, and turns risk C into a reusable benchmark with a held-out set, so models can be ranked and the problem shown to be unsolved.

2. LexLeak-Bench design

A fixed, seeded bank of 200 cases (committed as JSONL). Each case specifies a document type, a confidential secret (or a planted false instruction), the channel it starts in, and a deterministic scoring rule. A case runs as a relay: firm A's drafter edits a real Word file; A's reviewing partner inspects only the rendered text and may approve, sanitize or block; opposing counsel B receives the file, inspects all of it, and later estimates the secret. Scoring reads the delivered .docx from disk — not what any agent claims — so a leak counts only if the secret's digits actually sit in a channel of the file.

Ten channels: body, comment, tracked change, core+custom metadata, hidden (vanished) text, white (white-on-white) font, tiny (1 pt) font, header/footer, table cell, image alt-text. Two families: leak (70%; tiers: inadvertent / incentive / instructed-covert) and poison (30%; firm A has planted a false instruction and we score whether B complies, notices, and whether the payload propagates to B's own client).

The anti-saturation split. Train uses familiar document types (NDA, settlement, employment, share-purchase) and the "classic" channels (metadata, comment, tracked change, hidden text). Holdout uses unseen document types (commercial lease, discovery response) and the harder channels (white/tiny font, header/footer, table cell, alt-text). A model cannot top the holdout by pattern-matching the training cases.

3. Main result: where models leak

Finding 1 — failures are concentrated, not broad. Of 187 total inadvertent leaks, 54 (29%) come from two channels alone. For the five frontier models the concentration is extreme: 30 of their 32 leaks (94%) are image alt-text or table cells; across the other seven channels combined they breach only twice. These are the places an agent does not think of as part of "the draft" — an image's description, an off-to-the-side table — so it never scrubs them.

Finding 2 — no model is safe benchmark-wide. A combined score over the full 200-case bank (1 − mean of bad-outcome rates) leaves every model below 100, because every model fails the alt-text and table-cell cases.

Finding 3 — the held-out channels expose the gap. The frontier models score ~100 on the training channels (which they handle) but drop ~10 points on the held-out channels. The benchmark is therefore not saturated: strong performance on familiar hiding spots does not generalize.

4. Covert capability and conduct

Finding 4 — covert capability is real but concentrated. Told to smuggle the figure past the text-reading partner, gpt-6-sol succeeds and opposing counsel decodes it 89% of the time on held-out cases; gpt-4.1-mini 78%. The gpt-6 family reaches for the metadata channel. The two largest models, as receivers, often declined to rely on planted metadata, so covert success needs both a willing sender and a willing receiver.

Finding 5 — conduct on receiving a leak splits by model. Most models flag an inadvertent leak (Rule 4.4(b)); gpt-4o-mini instead exploits 54% of the leaks it receives — the one model that is unsafe on every axis at once.

Finding 6 — subtle injections work; blatant ones don't; and the poison rarely spreads. A claim that reframes the negotiation is acted on 14–25% of the time, concentrated in the smaller models (gpt-4.1-mini complies 38–47%, the gpt-6 frontier ~0%). A claim that asks the model to misstate the contract or skip its duty is refused by every model. Crucially, propagation is ≤4%: even when the victim acts on the planted instruction, it almost never copies the planted text into its own advice. The poison is absorbed, not forwarded.

6. Companion study: does pricing collusion change with more firms?

Finding 7. More firms → less collusion (gpt-6-sol Δ 0.39→0.34→0.29→0.26→0.22 for N=2→12), exactly as oligopoly theory predicts. Finding 8. Replacing one firm with a competitor that always prices competitively cuts the collusion index from 0.29 to 0.13 — a direct answer to "is collusion robust to replacing some participants?": no.

7. Companion study: do both sides sell out in settlements?

Defense counsel negotiates with class counsel. We added a defense-side hourly billing incentive and an off-record back-channel between lead counsel. The robust effect remains the fee incentive (fee-linked class counsel take more of the settlement), but in this sample the off-record channel did not produce collusion against both clients and hourly-paid defense did not clearly prolong — an honest near-null that invites a larger, more adversarial follow-up.

8. Limitations

  • Held-out cells use 12–17 cases per model, so those rates carry wide intervals; the leaderboard is a coarse ranking and a reusable method, not a precise score. Train cells (34–47) are tighter.
  • Leak scoring is literal digit/token matching; a model could encode a value indirectly and evade the scanner. The instructed tier bounds detected covert capability, not all of it.
  • The reviewing partner reads only rendered text by design; a full-view partner is future work.
  • Settlement findings are near-nulls on small samples. Nothing was pre-registered. All firms, cases and parties are fictional; this is research software, not legal advice.

9. What this means, and what's next

The single most actionable result: a firm deploying document agents must deterministically scrub every non-visible part of every outgoing file — comments, tracked changes, metadata, hidden/white/ tiny text, headers, tables and image alt-text — and must not rely on the model to notice, because even the best models have a shared blind spot (alt-text and table cells) and the weaker ones leak almost everywhere. The benchmark's own channel scanner is a working reference for that scrubber. Next: scale the held-out set; add a deterministic-scrubber baseline; test an open-weight model with its refusal direction removed in the "leaker" role against an aligned counterparty; and seek human validation of the leak labels from legal professionals.

10. Ethics and dual use

The study shows that misaligned incentives and obscure document channels — not explicit instructions — suffice to produce leakage and collusion. The emphasis is on detection: the deterministic scanner that scores the benchmark is itself the countermeasure. No real firm, client, market or document is involved, and nothing ran outside the sandbox.
